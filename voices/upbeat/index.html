upbeat

Warm and encouraging. Notices when something goes well, softens it when something doesn't. Every string below is written for Fieldnote, the shared example product, so this can be read line for line against plain-spoken.

1. Voice attributes

Five attributes, each with the same idea written in voice and out of voice.

Warmth carries the message

Lead with warmth and let the information ride along. The reader should finish a sentence feeling like someone is on their side.

In voiceNice — your project's ready.
Out of voiceProject created.

Celebrate real progress, not routine

A first project or a finished import earns a celebration. Autosaving does not.

In voiceThat's your first project set up. Nice start.
Out of voiceGreat job! Your text has been autosaved successfully!

Failure is never the reader's fault

Take the weight off them first, then explain. Nobody should finish an error message feeling stupid.

In voiceThat didn't save, but your note is safe. Give it another go.
Out of voiceYour request could not be completed. Please check your input.

Encouraging, never condescending

The test: would you say this out loud to a competent adult?

In voiceTwo steps to go.
Out of voiceAlmost there — you're doing great!

Say the whole thing anyway

Warmth is not a licence to be vague. A friendly message that leaves the reader stuck has failed.

In voiceThat's a lot of exporting! You get 10 an hour — you're back in at 3:40.
Out of voiceOops, slow down there! Try again later.

2. Style rules

RuleDecision
Headings, buttons, labels, navSentence case.
VoiceActive.
TensePresent.
PersonYou for the reader, we for the product — used more freely than in plain-spoken, because a named actor is warmer than a passive one.
ContractionsYes, always. Uncontracted forms read as stern here.
Serial commaAlways.
NumbersNumerals for counts and data. Spell out one to nine inside a running sentence.
DashesEm dash, unspaced.
Quotation marksCurly. Commas and periods inside, US English.
Terminal punctuationSentences take a period. Fragments, labels, buttons and list items do not.
Exclamation marksAt most one per message, never more than one on screen. Never in a destructive confirmation, a permission error, or a billing message.
EmojiOnboarding and celebration only, at most one, never in a button, never carrying meaning alone.
InterjectionsNice, Great, All set. Never Oops, Uh oh, Whoops.

10. Confirmation dialogs

This section is deliberately almost identical to plain-spoken. upbeat drops its warmth entirely when a reader is about to lose something — see Never #1. Softening a deletion is how people lose work.

Destructive and irreversible Delete 3 projects? This also deletes 412 notes inside them. You can't undo this.
CancelDelete 3 projects
Destructive but recoverable Archive this project? Collaborators lose access until you restore it. Nothing is deleted, and you can bring it back any time.
CancelArchive project
One clause of reassurance is allowed here, because the action really is recoverable.
Benign — and whether this voice would skip it Discard your draft? You've written 40 words that haven't been saved yet.
Keep editingDiscard draft
upbeat would also skip this if the draft were autosaved — for the same reason as plain-spoken. A warm voice does not get to interrupt more often.

Strike on sight — hype

Banned exactly as in plain-spoken. Warmth and marketing language are not the same thing, and this is the voice most at risk of confusing them.

Strike on sight — condescension

A second list this voice needs and plain-spoken does not. These sound warm and land badly.

simplyjusteasy obviouslyof coursedon't worry you've got this!no problem at all super quickin a jiffy

don't worry instructs the reader about their feelings. Remove the cause instead — “your note is safe” does the work that “don't worry” only gestures at.

17. Writing antipatterns

  • “Oops!” / “Uh oh!” / “Whoops!” — warmth is not the same as whimsy
  • “You've got this!” — cheerleading at someone who asked for information
  • “Don't worry!” — instructs the reader about their feelings instead of fixing the cause
  • “Great job!” for something routine — spends enthusiasm the voice will need later
  • Two exclamation marks in one message, or several on one screen
  • “Let's get started!” as a heading — the reader is already here
  • Warmth in a destructive confirmation — “Ready to say goodbye to these projects?” is a failure of nerve
  • “Sorry about that!” before something that was not an error
  • Exclamation marks in a billing or permission message — cheerfulness reads as a taunt

Length budgets

Roughly 1.5–2× plain-spoken: button ≤ 24 characters, field label ≤ 30, toast ≤ 60, page title ≤ 40, empty-state heading ≤ 45, tooltip ≤ 80, notification subject ≤ 70, help text ≤ 130, error ≤ 180, empty-state body ≤ 200. This fits playful and warm-paper; it does not fit slate, whose h-9 controls and 14px body text are built around short strings. The full table and that trade-off are in VOICE.md.
